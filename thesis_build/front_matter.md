# Abstract

The Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 (the "EU AI Act") classifies AI systems used to inform binding financial decisions as high-risk and, from 2 August 2026, requires their providers to deliver decision artefacts that a human supervisor can audit and override. The dominant explainability tool in finance, SHAP, is correlational by construction: it explains a model, not the world. This thesis ports the LEWIS counterfactual-explanation framework (Galhotra et al., 2021; Takahashi et al., 2024) to a real German equity-market setting and tests whether it delivers more useful and trustworthy explanations than SHAP, in the eyes of expert investors and against the EU AI Act's transparency requirements.

We train a CatBoost classifier on twenty DAX constituents over five years of monthly OHLCV data (n=960 ticker-months, nine features), recover a directed acyclic graph using DirectLiNGAM under a target-as-sink prior, compute LEWIS Necessity, Sufficiency and max-Necessity-Sufficiency scores via Pearl's backdoor adjustment, and benchmark them against SHAP TreeExplainer. The classifier reaches 0.974 accuracy on a chronological holdout. The Spearman rank correlation between LEWIS and SHAP feature rankings is 0.137 (p=0.599) — i.e. the two methods do not agree beyond chance. A live Streamlit instrument was used to collect twenty-five expert evaluations; among the eleven respondents who expressed a preference, eight (73 %) chose the LEWIS counterfactual. An OLS regression of trust on five explanation predictors yields R²=0.419 with F(5, 19)=2.743, p=0.0499; the only significant predictor is the respondent's own confidence (β=0.216, p=0.047), while the binary indicator for the counterfactual condition is null. We argue that the divergence between LEWIS and SHAP is precisely what Article 13 of the EU AI Act requires providers to surface, and that LEWIS-style counterfactual artefacts are a feasible compliance route for high-risk financial AI from August 2026.

**Keywords:** causal explainability, counterfactual explanation, LEWIS, SHAP, machine learning, investment decisions, EU AI Act, DAX, expert trust, causal discovery.
