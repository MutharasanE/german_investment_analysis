# Chapter 6: Discussion

The empirical exercise documented in Chapter 5 produced four findings worth interpreting against the literature reviewed in Chapter 2 and the regulatory anchor sketched in Chapter 1. First, LEWIS and SHAP disagree almost completely on the rank-ordering of feature importance for the German equity panel. Second, when expert respondents were forced to choose, they preferred the counterfactual artefact by a clear margin, but a majority refused to express a preference at all. Third, the only statistically significant predictor of trust was the respondent's own self-rated confidence, not the explanation type. Fourth, these results have direct, near-term implications for compliance with the EU AI Act, whose high-risk provisions become applicable on 2 August 2026. This chapter takes each of those findings in turn, asks whether it fits or contradicts existing theory, and then closes with the limitations the design imposes and the contribution that survives them.

## 6.1 LEWIS versus SHAP — what the divergence means

The headline number from Chapter 5 is a Spearman rank correlation of 0.137 between LEWIS and SHAP, with p = 0.599 (see §6.4 of Chapter 5). The two methods place the same feature at rank 1 — return_1y — and then go their separate ways. SHAP attributes roughly 0.55 of normalized importance to volatility and 0.62 to max_drawdown, whereas LEWIS, computed on the DirectLiNGAM DAG under prior (b), pushes both features down to ranks 7 and 6 respectively. Conversely, LEWIS lifts volume_avg and the macro driver ecb_rate above where SHAP would place them.

It is tempting to read this disagreement as a defect of one method or the other. We argue the opposite: the disagreement is exactly what (Pearl, 2009)'s ladder of causation predicts. SHAP, as formulated by (Lundberg and Lee, 2017), is an associational object — it decomposes a model's prediction into a sum of marginal contributions averaged over coalitions of features, and those marginals are computed against the empirical joint distribution. It lives on the bottom rung of Pearl's hierarchy. LEWIS, by construction, lives on the second and third rungs: necessity and sufficiency are interventional and counterfactual quantities, computed by backdoor adjustment on a discovered structural model (Galhotra et al., 2021). When a proximal mediator like return_1y lies between volatility and the decision label, the upstream variance carried by volatility flows almost entirely through that mediator. SHAP, blind to the DAG, will still hand volatility a large coalition share because the model's prediction does covary with it. LEWIS, conditioning on return_1y under do(volatility), correctly registers that volatility's *causal* leverage on the decision is small once the mediator is set. The methods give different answers because they are answering different questions. That is a feature, not a bug, and it is the central reason a finance practitioner who needs to reason about interventions cannot rely on SHAP alone.

The same logic explains why ecb_rate climbs the LEWIS ranking but not the SHAP one. Macro variables sit upstream of firm-level features in the discovered DAG; their effect on the decision flows through firm-level mediators. SHAP sees the diluted marginal at the leaf and rates it small. LEWIS sees the upstream lever and rates it material. To our knowledge, the 0.137 rank-correlation figure is the first such number reported on a real German equity panel — (Takahashi et al., 2024) report comparable disagreement on synthetic benchmarks, but the empirical generalization to a live market panel was, until now, an open question.

## 6.2 Expert preference for counterfactual explanations

Of the 25 evaluations collected, 11 expressed a methodological preference; of those 11, 8 chose the LEWIS counterfactual presentation, yielding a 73 percent preference rate among preference-expressing respondents (Chapter 5, §6.5). The remaining 14 selected "no preference". The 73 percent figure is a small-sample headline and we treat it accordingly, but it sits comfortably with the normative argument advanced by (Wachter et al., 2017) and the review by (Verma et al., 2020): counterfactual explanations match the way humans reason about agency, recourse, and "what would have had to be different". A portfolio manager evaluating a buy/hold recommendation already thinks in terms of conditional revisions — *if return_1y had been ten percentage points lower, would the model still have flagged this name?* — and a counterfactual artefact answers that question in its native idiom. A SHAP plot, however well-rendered, does not.

The 14/25 No-preference share deserves more weight than a quick read of the result might suggest, and §6.5 below revisits it explicitly as a limitation. Indifference among trained portfolio managers is not the same as ignorance; it is plausibly the rational response of a domain expert who can extract the same operational insight from either visualization once the underlying model and feature set are fixed. That observation softens any triumphalist reading of the 73 percent figure.

## 6.3 Trust drivers — the explanation type is not the lever

The OLS regression of trust on five explanation predictors (Chapter 5, §6.6) returned R² = 0.419, F(5, 19) = 2.743, p = 0.0499. Only one predictor crossed the conventional α = 0.05 threshold: confidence_score, with β = 0.216 and p = 0.047. The dummy is_counterfactual_b — the indicator that distinguishes a LEWIS explanation from a SHAP one — was almost exactly zero (β = 0.001, p = 0.998). Labelling an explanation "counterfactual" did not move the trust dial.

This is, on first reading, surprising. If 73 percent of preference-expressing respondents chose the counterfactual, why does counterfactual presentation not predict higher trust? The reconciliation is that *preference* and *trust* are different constructs and our design measured them separately. Preference asks which artefact a respondent would rather use; trust, on the 1-10 scale, asks how much weight the respondent would place on the *recommendation* the artefact accompanies. The mediating variable, on this evidence, is the respondent's own self-assessed confidence in their reading of the explanation. That is precisely the prediction made by (Lee and See, 2004) in their canonical treatment of trust in automation: appropriate reliance is not a function of the artefact's surface properties but of the user's calibrated competence with it. The result also echoes the broader review by (Glikson and Woolley, 2020), which finds that perceived comprehensibility, not nominal explanation type, drives trust in AI.

The negative — and statistically non-significant — coefficient on num_features (β = −0.243, p = 0.102) hints at a familiar cognitive-load story: more features in the explanation produced lower reported trust, holding other things constant. We do not lean on this coefficient, given the borderline p-value and the small sample, but the sign is in the direction (Doshi-Velez and Kim, 2017) and (Bhatt et al., 2020) would predict. Together, these results suggest the practical recommendation is not "ship counterfactuals because users prefer them" but rather "ship explanations the user can read confidently, in the smallest sufficient feature vector". Counterfactuals achieve that more naturally for some users, but the mechanism is comprehension, not the philosophical category of explanation.

## 6.4 Regulatory implications under the EU AI Act

The European AI Act, Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, entered into force on 1 August 2024, with the high-risk provisions becoming applicable on 2 August 2026 (European Union, 2024). AI systems that evaluate creditworthiness or otherwise inform binding decisions on natural persons in financial services fall under Annex III. Two articles bear directly on the choice between SHAP and LEWIS. Article 13 requires transparency sufficient for users to interpret outputs, including the "main parameters of the decision" (Article 13(3)(b)(iv)). Article 14 requires human oversight effective enough that a supervisor can intervene, override, or stop the system.

We argue that a correlational artefact does not, on a strict reading, satisfy Article 13(3)(b)(iv). The "main parameters of the decision" language presupposes a decision-relevant counterfactual: what would have had to be different for the system to reach a different output? SHAP cannot answer that question. It can quantify how strongly the model's prediction covaries with each feature in the empirical distribution; it cannot tell a supervisor whether intervening on a given feature would have flipped the recommendation. LEWIS, by construction, does. Under Pearl's backdoor adjustment on the discovered DAG, necessity and sufficiency scores are exactly the quantities a supervisor would need to defend or contest a decision in a human-oversight loop.

The practical claim, then, is the following. From August 2026, providers of high-risk financial AI in the European Union will need to log, per served decision, an artefact that supports interventional and counterfactual interrogation. Building such a log retroactively is expensive; building it once at deployment time is cheap. This thesis demonstrates a panel-level counterfactual log on a real German equity universe and an open survey instrument that captures expert reception of that log. The artefact is not yet a per-decision audit object — that is the natural next step — but the methodology generalizes directly. SHAP-only deployments will, on this argument, struggle against an Article 13 compliance test that takes the "main parameters" language seriously.

## 6.5 Limitations

Six limitations bound the strength of the claims above. First, the survey panel comprises 25 expert evaluations and the F-test for the trust regression sits at p = 0.0499 — a borderline result. The Jarque-Bera test rejects normality of the residuals at p < 0.001, so the textbook OLS standard errors should be treated as rough, and bootstrap confidence intervals are the recommended fix; we noted this in Chapter 5, §6.6, and reiterate it here. Coefficient inference, in particular for is_counterfactual_b and num_features, should be read as suggestive rather than confirmatory. Second, all 25 respondents shared the same role and tenure profile (portfolio manager, five years' experience). The panel is internally homogeneous, which sharpens domain expertise but means external validity to retail investors, risk officers, or compliance auditors is unestablished. Third, the 14/25 No-preference rate, discussed in §6.2, is itself a finding worth flagging as a limitation: many trained users find clean visualizations of LEWIS and SHAP equivalently useful, and explanation infrastructure — layout, interactivity, audit trail — may matter as much in practice as explanation philosophy. Fourth, the LEWIS scores and the 0.137 rank correlation are computed under a single causal-discovery specification, DirectLiNGAM with prior (b). A fully pre-registered multi-DAG sensitivity sweep across PC, RESIT, LiM, NOTEARS, and NOTEARS-MLP under priors {0, a, b} would tighten the rank-correlation claim; the figures from such a sweep are reserved for the appendix and future work. Fifth, the labelling rule investment_decision = 1 if (return_1y / volatility) > 0.5 is retrospective and Sharpe-style (cf. Sharpe, 1966); it is a tractable target for explanation, not a forward-looking trading signal, and any reader who interprets the model as an alpha generator misreads the design. Sixth, causal discovery is identifiability-bounded: the do-calculus on the recovered DAG (Pearl, 2009; Spirtes et al., 2000) yields valid LEWIS scores only insofar as the DAG itself is correct. We have no oracle that certifies it is.

## 6.6 Contribution restated

What survives those caveats is, we believe, a small but tangible step forward. First, this thesis is the first operationalization of LEWIS on a live German equity panel; (Takahashi et al., 2024) report the underlying method on synthetic benchmarks, but the empirical step to a real market is non-trivial and had not been taken. Second, the LEWIS-versus-SHAP rank correlation of 0.137 on the DAX universe is, to our knowledge, the first such number for the German market and provides a concrete reference point for any subsequent comparison. Third, the OLS-on-trust experiment is the first head-to-head trust regression, in finance, that contrasts counterfactual explanations with feature-importance explanations under matched presentation. Its central finding — that confidence, not explanation type, predicts trust — has practical consequences for how providers should design explanation interfaces under the EU AI Act. Fourth, the open Streamlit instrument and the public GitHub repository allow the entire pipeline to be replicated, extended, and stress-tested by other research groups, which is the form of contribution most likely to compound. The next chapter draws these threads together and sketches the agenda the limitations open up.
