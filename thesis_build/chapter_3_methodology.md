# Chapter 3: Methodology

## 3.1 Overview of the analytical pipeline

The empirical study reported in this thesis rests on a single, end-to-end pipeline that begins with raw market data and ends with a regression of expert trust on explanation properties. Conceptually, the pipeline can be read as six logical stages chained in series, each feeding the next: data acquisition; feature engineering and labeling; supervised model training; causal structure learning; explanation generation; and human evaluation followed by quantitative analysis of the responses. The architecture diagram of the system, sketched mentally as boxes and arrows, would show data sources on the left (yfinance for equity prices, the European Central Bank Statistical Data Warehouse for the macroeconomic series), flowing rightward into a feature-engineering block, then into a CatBoost classifier. From the trained classifier, two parallel arms extend: an upper arm carrying the model's predictions into a causal-discovery block, and from there into the LEWIS counterfactual scoring routine; and a lower arm sending the same model into a SHAP TreeExplainer that produces the correlational baseline. The two explanation streams then converge into a Streamlit survey dashboard that shows them side by side to expert respondents. The respondents' choices, ratings, and free-text comments are persisted in a MongoDB Atlas database (with a SQLite fallback when the connection is unavailable), and the final box on the right of the diagram is an ordinary least squares regression that estimates how much each property of an explanation predicts respondents' self-reported trust [See Figure 1].

The pipeline can equivalently be expressed as a sequence of thirteen concrete steps. First, five years of monthly open-high-low-close-volume data are pulled for thirty DAX constituents through `yfinance`. Second, four macroeconomic series — the ECB main refinancing rate, the EUR/USD reference rate, German harmonized inflation year-on-year, and the CBOE volatility index — are pulled at monthly frequency. Third, five firm-level features are engineered from the OHLCV panel: realized volatility, six-month price momentum, six-month rolling mean of monthly volume, twelve-month return, and twelve-month maximum drawdown. Fourth, the firm-level features are joined to the four macros to yield a nine-feature panel of n equal to roughly 960 ticker-months after the rolling-window NA drop. Fifth, the binary investment label is constructed: `investment_decision = 1` whenever the ratio of twelve-month return to volatility exceeds 0.5, and zero otherwise — a rough Sharpe-style threshold designed to produce a roughly balanced class distribution rather than to mimic any particular institutional decision rule. Sixth, every continuous feature is discretized into ten equal-frequency bins, with bin edges fitted on the training set only to avoid leakage. Seventh, a CatBoost classifier is trained with two hundred boosting iterations, depth six, fixed seed, and Logloss as the objective. Eighth, six causal-discovery algorithms are run on the discretized panel under three prior regimes. Ninth, a primary specification is selected — DirectLiNGAM under prior (b), in which the target node is constrained to be a sink so that no feature can be a child of the decision. Tenth, LEWIS counterfactual scores — Necessity, Sufficiency, and the maximum of the two over bin-pair contrasts — are computed for each feature using a Pearl backdoor adjustment evaluated on the discovered DAG. Eleventh, SHAP TreeExplainer values are computed on the same trained CatBoost model as a correlational baseline. Twelfth, the Streamlit dashboard described in section 3.6 is deployed and the A/B survey is administered. Thirteenth, twenty-five expert evaluations are collected and an OLS regression of trust on five explanation predictors is fitted, with diagnostics reported.

The remainder of this chapter elaborates each of those stages. Section 3.2 covers the predictive model. Section 3.3 surveys the six causal-discovery methods and motivates the choice of DirectLiNGAM under prior (b). Section 3.4 develops the LEWIS counterfactual score formally. Section 3.5 introduces SHAP and is explicit about why it is a correlational instrument despite being often read causally. Section 3.6 describes the survey instrument and the persistence layer. Section 3.7 specifies the OLS model on trust. Sections 3.8 and 3.9 describe the holdout evaluation protocol and the robustness checks.

## 3.2 Predictive model: CatBoost classifier

The predictive model at the centre of the pipeline is CatBoost, a gradient-boosted decision-tree library introduced by Prokhorenkova et al. (2018). CatBoost was selected for three concrete reasons. First, the algorithm's *ordered boosting* scheme is designed to address the prediction-shift bias that arises when the same observations are used both to estimate the residual gradient and to build the next tree; this is particularly important on small panels with overlapping time windows, where standard gradient boosting can leak information across the in-sample residuals. Second, CatBoost grows *oblivious* (symmetric) decision trees, in which every split at a given depth uses the same feature-threshold pair across all branches. Oblivious trees act as a soft regularizer — they reduce variance and produce decision rules whose paths are easy to enumerate, an advantage when one then wants to read off counterfactual perturbations in a small number of bins. Third, the library exposes a fast, exact TreeSHAP implementation through the `shap` package, which is convenient for the correlational baseline in section 3.5.

The hyperparameters used in this thesis are listed in Table 4 [See Table 4]. We deliberately chose conservative settings: 200 boosting iterations, tree depth six, no early stopping, Logloss as the objective, and a fixed `random_seed` for reproducibility. Verbose output is disabled. The class balance is approximately 50/50 by construction of the labeling threshold, so neither class re-weighting nor focal losses are applied.

A reader familiar with the practice of supervised learning may note that the model is trained on the full panel and that the in-sample accuracy is essentially perfect. This is not an oversight. The thesis is not making a claim about out-of-sample predictive performance; the contribution is methodological — a comparison of two explanation paradigms applied to the *same* trained classifier. A model that has memorised its training data is the cleanest object on which to demonstrate the difference between a correlational and a counterfactual explanation, because the predictions to be explained are not the noisy artefact of an underfit classifier. We nevertheless report a parallel holdout evaluation in section 3.8 so that the reader can verify that the model's behaviour on unseen DAX months is not pathological. On a time-based 80/20 split (the last twelve months held out) the classifier reaches accuracy 0.974, precision 1.000, and recall 0.959; under a random 80/20 split with seed 42 the accuracy is 0.990. These numbers are reported for completeness and are not the object of inference.

## 3.3 Causal discovery

Counterfactual scoring as developed in section 3.4 requires a directed acyclic graph (DAG) over the features and the target. Because no DAG is given to us a priori, the structure must be learned from the data. We ran six discovery algorithms that span the major families used in the recent literature, applied each under three increasingly restrictive prior specifications, and then chose a single primary specification that we use throughout the rest of the thesis.

The first algorithm is the **PC algorithm** of Spirtes et al. (2000), a constraint-based procedure that begins with a fully connected undirected graph and iteratively removes edges between variable pairs found conditionally independent on some subset of the remaining variables. The output is a CPDAG — a graph in which some edges are oriented and others remain undirected because the data alone do not identify their direction. PC is principled and assumption-light, but it relies on the *faithfulness* assumption that every conditional independence in the joint distribution corresponds to a missing edge in the true DAG, which can be uncomfortable on small samples.

The second algorithm is **DirectLiNGAM** (Shimizu et al., 2011), a method that exploits non-Gaussianity in the residuals to identify a fully directed linear structural-equation model in one pass, without iterative search. DirectLiNGAM assumes additive linear relationships and non-Gaussian disturbances; under those assumptions the directionality is fully identified, which is the property we exploit when we want a single DAG rather than an equivalence class.

The third algorithm is **RESIT** — regression with subsequent independence test — a non-linear extension of the additive-noise idea: for each candidate ordering, regress each variable on its predecessors and test residuals for independence from the predictors; the ordering whose residuals pass independence is the causal one. RESIT relaxes linearity but is computationally heavier and more sensitive to the choice of independence test on a small panel.

The fourth algorithm is **LiM** (Linear Mixed), an LiNGAM variant designed for panels in which some variables are continuous and others are discrete or binary. Because our investment-decision target is binary while the inputs are discretised but originate as continuous, LiM is a natural candidate.

The fifth and sixth algorithms come from the NOTEARS family. **NOTEARS** (Zheng et al., 2018) recasts structure learning as a continuous optimization problem with an algebraic acyclicity constraint, replacing the combinatorial search over DAGs with a smooth program solvable by gradient methods. **NOTEARS-MLP** generalises this to non-linear functional forms by parameterising each conditional with a small multilayer perceptron. We note that on our panel the `causal-learn` package's NOTEARS-MLP routine is not always stable, and the implementation we use falls back transparently to a GES search with the BIC score when the optimization fails to converge — a fact we report rather than hide.

Table 5 [See Table 5] summarises the six methods, their core distributional assumptions, and what kind of object each returns (oriented DAG, CPDAG, or partial DAG with undirected residuals).

For each method we ran three prior specifications. Prior **0** is uninformative — no edges are forbidden or required. Prior **a** forbids edges between macro variables and firm-level features in the wrong direction (it is implausible that a single firm's volatility moves the ECB main refinancing rate). Prior **b** combines prior **a** with a *target-as-sink* constraint: no edge may have the investment-decision node as its source. Prior (b) is the most restrictive but also the most defensible — by definition the binary decision is constructed *from* the features, so under our framing it cannot causally feed back into them within a single ticker-month. We adopt **DirectLiNGAM under prior (b)** as the primary specification used throughout the thesis. The discovered DAG over the nine features and the target node is shown in Figure 2 [See Figure 2]. The graph contains a small number of compact mediator paths: `return_1y` enters the decision directly; `volatility` and `max_drawdown` reach the decision largely through `return_1y` and through each other, which is the structural reason that LEWIS later attributes their effect upstream rather than directly. The macro variables sit at the root: `ecb_rate` and `de_inflation` flow into `eur_usd` and into the firm-level features, with `vix` acting as a separate exogenous shock. Section 3.9 returns to the question of how stable this graph is across the other five algorithms and the other two priors [See Figure 12].

## 3.4 Counterfactual scoring with LEWIS

With the predictive model from section 3.2 and the DAG from section 3.3 in hand, we can now state the counterfactual quantities that constitute the central explanation artefact of this thesis. The LEWIS framework, introduced by Galhotra et al. (2021) and extended for black-box models with learned causal structure by Takahashi et al. (2024), defines two contrastive probabilities from Pearl's (2009) structural counterfactual semantics.

Let `X` denote a feature, `Y` the target (the investment decision), `x` and `x'` two distinct values of `X`, and `y` the realised target value. The **Necessity** of feature value `x` for outcome `y` is

> N(x → y) = P(Y_{x'} ≠ y | X = x, Y = y),

i.e. the probability that, had the feature taken the alternative value `x'` instead of `x`, the outcome would have flipped, conditional on having actually observed `(X = x, Y = y)`. **Sufficiency** is the dual quantity:

> S(x → y) = P(Y_x = y | X ≠ x, Y ≠ y),

the probability that, had a unit who *did not* receive `x` and did not see outcome `y` instead been given `x`, the outcome would now be `y`. Necessity captures "the feature mattered to the cases that *did* receive it"; Sufficiency captures "the feature would have rescued the cases that did not".

Both probabilities involve a counterfactual `Y_{x}` — the outcome that *would* obtain under a forced setting of `X = x`. To evaluate that counterfactual on an observational panel we must contend with confounding through the rest of the DAG. We use the standard Pearl backdoor adjustment (Pearl, 2009): for a variable `X` with parents `Pa(X)` in the discovered DAG, the interventional distribution is

> P(Y | do(X = x)) = Σ_{z ∈ Pa(X)} P(Y | X = x, Pa(X) = z) · P(Pa(X) = z),

where `Pa(X)` is read off the DAG and the conditional `P(Y | X, Pa(X))` is estimated by querying the trained CatBoost model with the feature configuration and averaging predicted probabilities over the empirical distribution of `Pa(X)`. Discretising every continuous feature into ten equal-frequency bins, fixed on the training set, makes both the conditioning and the marginalisation tractable: the sum over `Pa(X)` reduces to a sum over the observed joint bin configurations.

Necessity and Sufficiency depend on the specific contrast `(x, x')`. To produce a single per-feature scalar that we can present to a respondent, we follow Takahashi et al. (2024) and report the **maximum Necessity-Sufficiency** (max-Nesuf) score, defined as the maximum of `max(N, S)` taken over a low-bin / high-bin pair contrast; concretely we contrast the lowest-decile bin against the highest-decile bin of each feature. The max-Nesuf score is bounded in [0,1] and can be normalized within a feature set so that the most influential feature scores 1.000. The normalized rankings reported in Chapter 5 follow this convention.

A small implementation note. The codebase contains two routines that implement the above arithmetic — a counterfactual evaluator (`src/lewis.py`) and a backdoor estimator (`src/backdoor.py`) — and both operate on the `(model, DAG, binned panel)` triple in a way that makes the substitution of a different DAG or a different model entirely modular. This modularity matters for Chapter 5, where we rerun the same scoring routine under several alternative DAGs to assess robustness.

The interpretive payoff of LEWIS is direct. Where SHAP answers "how much does the model rely on this feature?", LEWIS answers "if this feature had been at its low bin instead of its high bin, would the model's decision have flipped?". The latter is the question that Article 13 of the EU AI Act expects a high-risk system's documentation to be able to answer, and it is the question a portfolio manager naturally asks when probing a recommendation.

## 3.5 SHAP as a correlational baseline

The natural comparator for any feature-attribution method is SHAP — Shapley Additive exPlanations — introduced by Lundberg and Lee (2017). SHAP allocates the deviation of an individual prediction from the model's mean output to the input features by computing the Shapley value of each feature in the cooperative game in which features collaborate to produce the prediction. For tree ensembles, the `TreeExplainer` algorithm computes Shapley values exactly (rather than by sampling) by recursing through the tree structure, which makes SHAP both fast and reproducible on CatBoost models.

We compute SHAP values on the same trained CatBoost classifier used for LEWIS, for the same 960-row panel, and report mean absolute SHAP per feature. The output is a per-feature scalar in the same units as the log-odds of the model output, which we normalize so that the most influential feature is at 1.000. Crucially, SHAP is included in this thesis not as a competitor we wish to dismiss but as the *correlational baseline* against which the counterfactual artefact is to be evaluated. Most production explainability stacks use SHAP, so it is the natural frame of reference for any practitioner audience.

A point of methodological care, often glossed over in industry deployments. SHAP values are sometimes read as if they were causal. They are not. A SHAP value tells the analyst *how the model uses* a feature; it is silent on whether the feature itself causally drives the outcome. If two features are correlated and only one of them genuinely causes the outcome, the model — fitting in a least-loss sense — will distribute attribution between them, and SHAP will faithfully reflect that distribution. The interpretation "feature `j` matters for the outcome" is therefore a *conjunction* of two claims: the model uses `j`, and the model itself is correctly specified causally. The first claim is what SHAP supplies. The second claim is what the discovery-then-counterfactual pipeline of section 3.4 supplies. This is the conceptual gap that the thesis, and the survey instrument described next, are designed to expose to the expert respondent.

## 3.6 Survey instrument

The expert evaluation arm of the pipeline is a Streamlit web application deployed at `https://germaninvestmentanalysis.streamlit.app`. The application's task is to obtain, from each respondent, a structured comparison of the LEWIS counterfactual artefact and the SHAP correlational artefact for one randomly chosen DAX ticker drawn from the 20-ticker universe of section 4.2.

When a respondent opens the app they first land on a brief consent-and-context screen that introduces the binary investment decision and the role of the explanation. After consenting, the respondent is shown two side-by-side panels. Both panels relate to the same ticker and the same model prediction, but they present different artefacts. Panel A displays the SHAP summary in a horizontal bar chart with the top features in descending order of mean absolute attribution, accompanied by a short verbal gloss identifying it as a "feature-importance" view. Panel B displays the LEWIS view: the top three to five features by max-Nesuf score, each annotated with an interpretable counterfactual sentence ("If `return_1y` had been in its lowest decile instead of its highest decile, the model's decision would have flipped with probability 0.94"). The labels A and B are randomised across respondents so that ordering effects do not bias the measurement.

The respondent is then asked four primary questions and one open-ended one. The primary questions are: *which explanation do you prefer, A or B (or "no preference")*; *how much do you trust the model's recommendation given this explanation, on a 1–10 scale*; *how confident are you that you understand the explanation, on a 1–10 scale*; and *how mechanically accurate does the explanation read — highly accurate, somewhat accurate, or theoretical*. The open-ended question collects free-text comments. An attention-check item is interleaved with the primary questions: it asks the respondent to identify which of two clearly distinct features was named in the explanation panel they are commenting on, and the response is later coded as an `attention_flag` (1 = passed; 0 = failed) that we use as a covariate in the trust regression of section 3.7.

Persistence is implemented in two layers. The primary store is MongoDB Atlas, which each completed response writes into a single collection keyed by a session UUID. To make the application robust to transient connectivity issues — including the case where the live demo is opened from a network behind a firewall — a SQLite fallback writes the same response schema to a local file; a periodic merge harmonises the two stores into the analysis dataset. The data model is intentionally flat: one row per completed response, with columns for each rating, the chosen panel, the attention-check result, the timestamp, and a free-text comment field.

The `is_counterfactual_b` indicator that enters the regression below is constructed from the chosen panel: it equals 1 when the respondent preferred the LEWIS panel and 0 otherwise (no-preference responses contribute 0). The `accuracy_score` field is a numerical encoding of the mechanical-accuracy item (1 = theoretical, 2 = somewhat, 3 = highly accurate). The `num_features` field counts the features actually displayed in the explanation the respondent commented on, which differs by design between SHAP (always nine) and LEWIS (top three to five by max-Nesuf).

## 3.7 OLS regression on trust

The central inferential question of the thesis is whether the *kind* of explanation a respondent is shown — counterfactual or feature-importance — predicts how much that respondent trusts the model. We address this with an ordinary least squares regression of the self-reported trust score on five predictors, fitted with `statsmodels.OLS` on the n = 25 evaluations collected through the survey:

> trust_score_i = β_0 + β_1 · is_counterfactual_b_i + β_2 · accuracy_score_i + β_3 · num_features_i + β_4 · confidence_score_i + β_5 · attention_flag_i + ε_i

The choice of OLS over a more elaborate model is deliberate. With twenty-five observations any latent-variable or mixed-effects specification is severely under-identified; OLS yields coefficients on the original scale of the rating items, which are interpretable as the change in trust associated with a one-unit change in the predictor. We explicitly report adjusted R-squared, the F statistic with its p-value, and Durbin-Watson and Jarque-Bera statistics for the residuals.

Two diagnostic concerns deserve flagging in advance. First, with n = 25 the OLS standard errors rest on asymptotic approximations that may not hold; we therefore recommend that any inferential claim from this regression be accompanied by bootstrap confidence intervals, a recommendation we follow in Chapter 5. Second, the Jarque-Bera test on the residuals of our fitted model returns p < 0.001, indicating non-normality, while the Durbin-Watson statistic of 2.396 is well within the no-autocorrelation band; the non-normality reinforces the case for bootstrap CIs over the t-tabulated standard errors.

The full coefficient table is presented and discussed in Chapter 5; for the purposes of the methodology chapter the relevant claim is that the specification above identifies one main effect of interest (`β_1`, the LEWIS-vs-SHAP indicator) and four control covariates, with sample-size limits acknowledged.

## 3.8 Holdout evaluation protocol

Although the thesis's central claims concern explanation quality rather than predictive accuracy, we still need to confirm that the trained CatBoost model is not pathological — that it generalises in the ordinary sense to data it did not see during training. We adopted two holdout protocols, run independently.

The **time-split** protocol holds out the last twelve months of the 60-month window as a contiguous test set, reserving the first 48 months as the training set. This split respects the temporal ordering of the panel and yields a test set of 192 rows (12 months × 20 tickers minus the rolling-window NA drop). Because CatBoost was re-fitted only on the training sub-panel for this evaluation, the metrics are a clean test of forward-looking generalisation.

The **random-split** protocol takes a 20 % uniform sample of the panel without regard to time, with `random_state` fixed at 42 to ensure reproducibility. This protocol does not respect the temporal structure and is included only as a sanity check that within-period generalisation behaves sensibly.

For both splits we report accuracy, precision, recall, F1, ROC-AUC, and a calibration curve. The full table of holdout metrics is Table 6, with Figure 7 showing the time-split confusion matrix and Figure 8 the calibration plot. The headline numbers are accuracy 0.974, precision 1.000, recall 0.959 on the time split, and accuracy 0.990 on the random split. The fact that the time split is slightly worse than the random split is the expected pattern when there is mild non-stationarity in the macro environment between the training and the test periods.

## 3.9 Robustness and stability

The pipeline contains several discretionary choices — the discovery algorithm, the prior, the binning, the winsorisation thresholds — and we now describe the checks that guard the substantive results against those choices.

The first and most important robustness check is **DAG stability**. We re-ran the LEWIS scoring under each of the eighteen combinations of {PC, DirectLiNGAM, RESIT, LiM, NOTEARS, NOTEARS-MLP} × {prior 0, prior a, prior b} and recorded the per-feature max-Nesuf score under each configuration. The pairwise rank correlation of these eighteen feature rankings is the substance of Figure 12 [See Figure 12], which shows that the methods cluster broadly into a "linear / SEM" group (DirectLiNGAM, LiM, NOTEARS) whose rankings are mutually consistent and a "constraint-based" group (PC) that produces a less directional but compatible ordering. The non-linear methods (RESIT, NOTEARS-MLP) sit between the two clusters and are the most volatile across priors. The headline conclusion — that `return_1y` is the dominant feature and that `volatility` and `max_drawdown` are demoted relative to their SHAP rank — holds across all eighteen configurations.

The second check is **performance stratification**. We ask whether the headline accuracy numbers of section 3.8 mask material variation across sectors or macroeconomic regimes. Figure 10 reports accuracy by sector for the 20 DAX tickers, partitioned by the GICS sector code; Figure 11 reports accuracy by macroeconomic regime, where the regime is a tertile of the realised VIX over the test window. If the model were systematically failing on, say, energy stocks during high-VIX months, the explanation methodology would inherit that failure and we would want to report it openly.

The third check concerns **outlier handling and stationarity**. The five firm-level features were winsorised at the 1st and 99th percentiles before binning, to prevent a single extreme month from collapsing all probability mass into the boundary bins. ADF tests were run on each input feature (Table 3); seven of the nine features are stationary at level, and the two macro variables that fail the test — `ecb_rate` and `de_inflation` — were first-differenced before being passed to the discovery algorithms, with the original-level series preserved as a covariate for the predictive model. This bifurcation between the model's input and the discovery's input is intentional: causal discovery on a non-stationary series invites spurious orientations, while the predictive model can reasonably consume the level if it is doing pattern recognition rather than structural inference.

The fourth and final check is the **survey-instrument robustness**. The `attention_flag` covariate in the trust regression isolates respondents who passed the embedded attention check from those who did not. The OLS coefficients reported in Chapter 5 are computed on the full sample, with `attention_flag` entered as a covariate; we additionally report the regression on the attention-pass subsample as a robustness check.

Taken together, sections 3.2 to 3.9 specify the methodology end to end. The chapter that follows — Chapter 4, *Data and Empirical Setup* — describes the data sources, sample windows, and preprocessing decisions in greater detail, before Chapter 5 reports the empirical findings.
