# Chapter 2: Literature Review

This chapter situates the thesis at the intersection of four research streams that have until recently developed in relative isolation: machine learning in empirical finance, post-hoc explainable artificial intelligence (XAI), causal inference and discovery, and the human-factors literature on trust in automated systems. A fifth stream — the emerging European regulatory regime for high-risk AI — now binds the others together and lends the discussion its institutional urgency. The chapter proceeds from finance to interpretation to causation to trust, and closes by translating those debates into the explicit research gap that motivates the empirical work in Chapters 3 to 6.

## 2.1 Machine learning in finance

The use of statistical models to explain and predict asset returns is older than computing itself, but the past decade has seen a decisive shift from linear factor models toward flexible, high-capacity machine learners. The classical baselines remain the reference point against which any new method is measured. Sharpe (1966) introduced reward-to-variability as the canonical measure of risk-adjusted performance and, in doing so, fixed the lens through which equity strategies are still evaluated today. Fama and French (1993) extended the single-factor capital asset pricing model into a multi-factor framework that incorporated size and book-to-market premia, and Jegadeesh and Titman (1993) documented the cross-sectional momentum anomaly that has resisted both theoretical absorption and arbitrage. These three studies together codified the working hypothesis that returns are driven by a small number of identifiable risk factors and that any successful prediction model must, at minimum, dominate them out of sample.

Modern machine learning challenges that hypothesis on two fronts. First, the predictor set is no longer hand-picked: gradient-boosted trees and deep networks ingest hundreds of firm characteristics and macroeconomic series and let the algorithm decide which interactions matter. Second, the functional form is no longer linear: nonlinearities and high-order interactions are absorbed by the model rather than discarded as residual. Gu, Kelly and Xiu (2020), in the most cited synthesis of the field, evaluated a battery of ML estimators on US equity returns from 1957 to 2016 and reported that tree ensembles and neural networks roughly doubled the out-of-sample monthly R-squared of the best linear baseline. The economic significance was not subtle — long–short portfolios formed on ML-implied expected returns delivered Sharpe ratios well above the Fama–French benchmarks. The verdict was that ML is not merely a faster regression; it is a different way of using information.

The enthusiasm has been accompanied by a sober counter-literature on what can go wrong. Lopez de Prado (2018) catalogued the failure modes of financial machine learning with unusual candor: overfitting under cross-validation that ignores the temporal structure of returns, p-hacking via undisclosed feature search, mistaken assumptions of independence across observations, and the publication bias inherent in factor-zoo journals. The book's central methodological injunction — that any ML pipeline applied to asset prices must be evaluated under a temporally honest holdout — has become close to canonical. The present thesis takes that injunction seriously: the CatBoost (Prokhorenkova et al., 2018) classifier described in Chapter 3 is evaluated on both a temporally ordered holdout and a random-split holdout, and the comparison is reported transparently in Chapter 5.

The dominant industrial-grade learners in finance today are gradient-boosted decision tree ensembles. CatBoost, LightGBM, and XGBoost share the same core idea — additive trees fitted to the residuals of previous trees — and routinely outperform deep networks on tabular panels, especially when the sample size is in the low thousands of observations rather than the millions. They are, however, opaque. A trained CatBoost model on a nine-feature panel may be a sum of two hundred trees of depth six, which translates to several thousand decision rules whose joint behavior no human can survey. Hence the interpretability problem.

## 2.2 The interpretability problem

Interpretability is not a single property but a family of partly overlapping desiderata, and the literature reflects that ambiguity. Doshi-Velez and Kim (2017) offered the conceptual framing now most widely cited: interpretability is a means, not an end, and its rigorous study requires distinguishing between application-grounded, human-grounded, and functionally grounded evaluation. Their argument matters because it forces researchers to specify *why* an explanation is needed before specifying *which* method to use. An explanation that satisfies a debugging engineer may be useless to a portfolio manager facing a regulator, and vice versa.

Arrieta et al. (2020) provided the most systematic taxonomy of XAI methods to date, sorting techniques along three axes: transparency of the underlying model, scope of the explanation (global versus local), and the type of artefact produced (feature attributions, rule sets, prototypes, surrogate models, counterfactuals). Their survey makes clear that the field is methodologically heterogeneous and that the term "explanation" hides important distinctions. A SHAP value and a counterfactual are not two flavors of the same dish; they answer different questions.

The most uncomfortable contribution to the debate comes from Rudin (2019), who argued that the very project of post-hoc explanation of black-box models for high-stakes decisions is misguided. Her position is that interpretable models — sparse linear models, generalized additive models, scoring systems — are usually competitive with black boxes on tabular problems, and that the marginal accuracy gain from a black box rarely justifies the loss of direct auditability. Rudin's challenge has been partly absorbed by the field: there is now broader acceptance that "explanation" should be a serious technical claim, not a comfort blanket draped over an opaque model. The position taken in this thesis is more pragmatic. CatBoost is used because it is the model practitioners actually deploy on tabular financial panels; the question we ask is not "should you use a black box?" but "given that black boxes are deployed, what kind of explanation does a regulator and a portfolio manager actually need?"

Bhatt et al. (2020) supplied a useful corrective on the deployment side. Surveying organizations actively using XAI, they found that explanations are most often consumed by internal engineers for debugging rather than by end users for decision support, and that user-facing explanations frequently fail to communicate the uncertainty around a prediction. The lesson is that an explanation method's value cannot be inferred from its mathematical elegance; it must be demonstrated against a concrete user, task, and domain. That observation directly motivates the expert survey reported in Chapter 5.

## 2.3 Post-hoc explainability methods

Two methods dominate post-hoc XAI in practice: LIME and SHAP. Ribeiro, Singh and Guestrin (2016) introduced LIME — local interpretable model-agnostic explanations — by fitting a sparse linear surrogate to the predictions of any classifier in the local neighborhood of an instance. The contribution was conceptual as well as algorithmic: it argued that local fidelity, not global fidelity, is the right desideratum for an explanation, and that any model can be explained without privileged access to its internals. LIME's weakness, well documented in subsequent work, is its sensitivity to the perturbation distribution and the surrogate's regularization, which can produce explanations that vary across runs of the same algorithm on the same instance.

Lundberg and Lee (2017) addressed that instability with SHAP — Shapley additive explanations — which assigns each feature an attribution equal to its average marginal contribution to the prediction across all coalitions of features. SHAP inherits from cooperative game theory the only attribution scheme satisfying local accuracy, missingness, and consistency simultaneously, and TreeSHAP delivers exact Shapley values for tree ensembles in polynomial time. SHAP has become the de facto interpretability tool in financial ML; it is the method against which any new explanation technique is compared, and it is the correlational baseline for this thesis.

The pragmatic value of SHAP is real. It quantifies which features the model relies on, surfaces interaction effects, and produces a global feature ordering that practitioners find intuitive. But it has a foundational limit, and that limit is the fulcrum of the present work. SHAP attributes the *model's* output to its inputs; it does not attribute the *world's* outcome to its causes. If two features are highly correlated and one is a true causal driver while the other is a downstream proxy, SHAP will distribute attribution between them according to the model's learned weights, not according to their causal role. Equivalently, SHAP cannot answer the question that regulators and portfolio managers most often want answered: *if this feature had been different, would the decision have flipped?* That is a counterfactual question, and counterfactual questions require a causal model.

## 2.4 Causal inference foundations

The modern formal treatment of causation begins with Pearl (2009), who set out the do-calculus and the ladder of causation. The ladder has three rungs: association (seeing — the level at which correlation, regression, and most of statistics operate), intervention (doing — the level of randomized controlled trials and the do-operator), and counterfactuals (imagining — the level of "what would have happened if"). Pearl's central methodological claim is that the rungs cannot be climbed by accumulating more data alone; each rung requires assumptions that the data themselves cannot validate. Specifically, the move from association to intervention requires identifying a directed acyclic graph (DAG) over the variables, and the move from intervention to counterfactuals additionally requires a structural model with specified functional forms.

How might a DAG be obtained when expert knowledge is incomplete? Causal discovery is the answer. Spirtes, Glymour and Scheines (2000) developed the constraint-based PC algorithm, which begins with a complete graph and iteratively removes edges between variables that become conditionally independent given some subset of the remaining variables. PC recovers a Markov-equivalence class of DAGs under two assumptions: the *causal Markov condition* (each variable is independent of its non-descendants given its parents) and *faithfulness* (the only conditional independencies in the data are those entailed by the graph). A third assumption — *causal sufficiency*, the absence of unobserved common causes — is required for any guarantee that the discovered structure has a causal interpretation.

PC produces an equivalence class, not a unique DAG. Shimizu et al. (2011) showed that uniqueness can be recovered if we add a distributional assumption: if the data-generating process is linear and the noise terms are non-Gaussian, then the DAG is fully identified, and a direct estimation algorithm — DirectLiNGAM — can recover it without iterative search. The non-Gaussianity assumption is not innocuous, but it is mild enough to be plausible for financial returns, which are well known to depart from Gaussianity in both tails and central peakedness. DirectLiNGAM is the primary discovery method used in this thesis.

A more recent line of work casts causal discovery as a continuous optimization problem rather than a combinatorial one. Zheng et al. (2018) introduced NOTEARS, which encodes acyclicity as a smooth equality constraint on the matrix exponential of the weighted adjacency matrix, allowing structure learning by gradient descent rather than by exhaustive permutation search. The technique scales gracefully and accommodates nonlinear extensions (NOTEARS-MLP), at the cost of greater sensitivity to hyperparameter choices and a weaker theoretical identifiability guarantee than DirectLiNGAM offers in its linear non-Gaussian regime.

The point of cataloguing these methods is not to choose a winner in the abstract but to motivate the *prior-and-method grid* used in Chapter 3. Different discovery algorithms encode different identifiability assumptions, and cross-method agreement on a recovered structure is itself evidence that the structure is not an artefact of any single algorithm. The thesis runs PC, DirectLiNGAM, RESIT, LiM, NOTEARS, and NOTEARS-MLP over three priors and reports the stability heatmap as part of its causal-validity argument.

## 2.5 Counterfactual explanations

Counterfactual explanations, as a distinct XAI primitive, were introduced into the legal and computer-science literatures by Wachter, Mittelstadt and Russell (2017). Their motivation was explicitly regulatory: the General Data Protection Regulation's right to "meaningful information about the logic involved" in automated decision-making could be operationalized, they argued, by providing the smallest change to the input that would have flipped the output. A counterfactual explanation is therefore action-guiding (it tells the subject what to change), regulator-friendly (it answers the lawful question of why a particular decision was made), and model-agnostic (it requires only black-box query access). Wachter et al. introduced the basic optimization formulation — minimize a distance from the factual instance subject to flipping the prediction — that has been the template for subsequent work.

Verma et al. (2020) reviewed the subsequent methods literature and organized it along several axes: feasibility constraints (which features can plausibly change), actionability (which changes are within the user's control), diversity (whether multiple counterfactuals are returned), and causal validity (whether the counterfactual respects the causal structure of the data). The last axis is the most recent and the least mature. Most counterfactual generators perturb features as if they were independently manipulable, which is statistically convenient but causally false: changing a firm's twelve-month return without changing its momentum, volatility, and drawdown is not a coherent intervention, because in the data-generating process those features move together. A counterfactual that ignores this is a counterfactual on the model, not on the world.

Galhotra, Pradhan and Salimi (2021) addressed exactly that gap. Their LEWIS framework defines three causal scores per feature, computed on a discovered DAG using Pearl's backdoor adjustment. *Necessity* is the probability that the outcome would have flipped had the feature been set to a counterfactual reference value, conditional on its observed value. *Sufficiency* is the probability that the outcome would have been observed had the feature been set to its observed value, conditional on a baseline. *Maximum Necessity-Sufficiency* (max-Nesuf) combines the two into a single contrastive importance score. Crucially, the backdoor adjustment over the DAG means that downstream descendants of a perturbed feature are propagated through the structural model rather than held constant — the counterfactual is causally coherent.

Takahashi et al. (2024), the paper that most directly inspired the present work, ported the LEWIS framework to a wider class of tabular ML problems and integrated it with causal discovery so that the DAG need not be supplied by an expert. Their pipeline is the methodological skeleton this thesis adopts and adapts: train a tabular learner, run causal discovery on the same feature panel, compute LEWIS scores via backdoor adjustment, and present them alongside SHAP for comparison. Takahashi et al. demonstrated the approach on UCI benchmark datasets; the contribution of the present thesis is to take it to a domain where it has not yet been validated — the German equity panel — and to evaluate its reception not by a machine-learning audience but by the financial professionals who would actually consume the explanations.

## 2.6 Trust and reliance on automation

The empirical work in Chapter 5 measures expert trust in causal versus correlational explanations. The construct of trust deserves a brief grounding in the human-factors literature, because its measurement and its interpretation are not self-evident. Mayer and Davis (1999) developed and validated the most widely used trust-in-management instrument, decomposing trust into three antecedents — perceived ability, integrity, and benevolence — and treating it as a willingness to be vulnerable to the trustee. Although the instrument was developed for organizational settings, its three-factor structure has been recycled across domains, including human–automation interaction.

Lee and See (2004) carried that decomposition into the automation context and articulated the now-canonical concept of *appropriate reliance*. Their argument is that the goal of human–automation system design is not to maximize trust but to calibrate it: users should rely on the automation when it is competent and override it when it is not. Miscalibration in either direction is costly. Over-trust produces complacency and undetected automation failures; under-trust produces unnecessary manual workload and the abandonment of useful tools. Importantly for the present thesis, Lee and See identified explanation transparency as one of the principal levers by which trust is calibrated — explanations that surface the automation's actual competence enable users to extend or withdraw trust appropriately.

Glikson and Woolley (2020), in the most recent comprehensive review of empirical work on trust in artificial intelligence, synthesized findings across more than 150 studies. Two of their conclusions matter here. First, the form of an explanation matters at least as much as its existence: explanations that are perceived as causal and mechanistic increase trust more reliably than explanations that are perceived as merely associative. Second, expertise moderates the relationship: domain experts evaluate AI explanations more critically than novices and are less easily swayed by superficial cues. Both conclusions raise the stakes of the empirical comparison in Chapter 5 — the respondents are portfolio managers with five years of experience, exactly the population least likely to be impressed by an explanation that only looks rigorous.

## 2.7 The EU AI Act and the regulatory turn

The institutional context for explainability shifted decisively in 2024. Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 on Artificial Intelligence — the so-called EU AI Act — entered into force on 1 August 2024, with high-risk provisions becoming applicable on 2 August 2026 (European Union, 2024). The Regulation operates by classifying AI systems into four risk tiers and assigning obligations accordingly. AI systems used to evaluate the creditworthiness of natural persons, or otherwise to make or inform binding decisions about them in financial services, fall under Annex III as high-risk.

Two articles bear directly on the present thesis. Article 13 obliges providers of high-risk AI systems to ensure that operation is sufficiently transparent to allow deployers to interpret outputs and use them appropriately, and specifically requires that the main parameters of the decision be communicated. Article 14 requires that high-risk systems be designed for effective human oversight, meaning that a human supervisor must be able to understand the system's outputs sufficiently to override them in operation. Both articles are deliberately silent on the technical means by which transparency and oversight are to be achieved; they specify what must be communicated, not how.

The thesis's argument with respect to the Regulation is that SHAP, as a correlational method, satisfies the transparency requirement only loosely. SHAP can answer "which features did the model rely on?", but it cannot answer "if this feature had been different, would the decision have flipped?". The latter question is the natural reading of a regulator asking what the *main parameters of the decision* were, and it is what a human supervisor exercising Article 14 oversight would actually need to override the system meaningfully. A counterfactual artefact constructed on a discovered causal graph — LEWIS — answers that question directly. This is not a doctrinal claim about how courts will eventually read Article 13; it is a methodological claim that causal counterfactuals are a more responsive technical answer to the spirit of the obligation than correlational attributions are.

## 2.8 Research gap

Drawing the four streams together produces a research gap that is sharper than any single stream would suggest. First, machine learning in finance has consolidated around opaque tree ensembles whose predictive performance is good enough that practitioners will not abandon them, and which are routinely deployed on tabular firm-and-macro panels of exactly the kind this thesis studies. Second, the dominant explanation technique used to make those models palatable — SHAP — is correlational by construction and cannot answer counterfactual questions about specific decisions. Third, a causal-counterfactual alternative exists in the form of the LEWIS framework (Galhotra et al., 2021), which Takahashi et al. (2024) demonstrated can be paired with causal discovery to operate on real tabular ML pipelines, but it has not been validated on a German equity panel and, critically, has not been evaluated by the financial professionals who would actually consume its outputs. Fourth, the EU AI Act's high-risk regime becomes applicable on 2 August 2026 and will require providers of financial AI systems to deliver explanations that a human supervisor can use to interrogate and override decisions — a requirement that fits causal counterfactuals more naturally than feature attributions.

The gap, stated simply, is the absence of empirical evidence on whether causal counterfactual explanations improve the trust and decision quality of expert German-market investors relative to the SHAP baseline they currently consume, on a panel and a model representative of actual deployment, in a regulatory environment that is about to demand precisely such causal justification. Filling that gap is the task of the remainder of the thesis. Chapter 3 specifies the methodology; Chapter 4 documents the data and empirical setup; Chapter 5 reports the results of both the algorithmic comparison and the expert survey; and Chapters 6 and 7 discuss what the findings imply for practice, regulation, and further work.
