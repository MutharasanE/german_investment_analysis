# Chapter 4: Data and Empirical Setup

This chapter documents the empirical foundation on which the rest of the thesis is built. It moves deliberately from the raw data sources, through the construction of the nine-feature panel, to the diagnostic checks, the discretisation step, the labelling rule, the partitioning scheme, and finally the survey instrument that supplies the human-evaluation data analysed in Chapter 5. The goal throughout is replicability: every choice is recorded with the exact window, library, transformation, and threshold so that an independent reader can rebuild the panel from the public sources alone.

## 4.1 Data sources and equity universe

The equity universe is a fixed cross-section of twenty constituents of the German DAX index. The tickers, in the order used internally by the pipeline, are SAP.DE, SIE.DE, ALV.DE, DTE.DE, BAS.DE, MBG.DE, BMW.DE, MUV2.DE, AIR.DE, IFX.DE, ADS.DE, DB1.DE, RWE.DE, HEN3.DE, VOW3.DE, SY1.DE, BEI.DE, EOAN.DE, FRE.DE, and MTX.DE [See Table 1]. No ESG screen, no size cut, and no liquidity filter were applied; the only criterion was index membership over the full sample window. This deliberately avoids the survivorship-style biases that creep in when filtering for "investable" or "ESG-eligible" subsets, at the cost of a moderately heterogeneous sectoral mix that the discovery step then has to disentangle.

The choice of N = 20 reflects a methodological compromise rather than a financial one. Causal-discovery algorithms over a nine-feature panel converge reliably with several hundred ticker-month observations; twenty constituents observed over six years deliver roughly that many rows after the rolling-window NA drop. Restricting to twenty also keeps DAG estimation within the regime in which the score-based methods used in Chapter 3 remain numerically well-behaved, while still spanning the major DAX sectors (industrials, financials, consumer goods, utilities, technology, healthcare, automotive). A larger universe would dilute the per-firm signal without materially expanding the variance of the macro covariates, which are common to all tickers.

The data window runs from January 2019 to December 2024 inclusive. Six years of monthly observations is a deliberate compromise between sample size and economic relevance. The interval brackets two clearly distinct macro-regimes: the pre-pandemic disinflationary phase, the COVID-19 drawdown of early 2020, the post-pandemic recovery, and the ECB tightening cycle that began in mid-2022. A model that explains decisions across this window therefore confronts both crisis behaviour and normalisation behaviour, and any causal structure that survives both regimes is more credible than one fitted on a single phase. We do not extend the window further back because the DAX composition pre-2019 differs substantially from the modern index, and because the macro series we use as upstream drivers are most uniformly available at monthly frequency from 2019 onward.

Monthly OHLCV data for the twenty equities were retrieved through the `yfinance` Python interface to Yahoo Finance. Monthly bars were used directly rather than aggregated from daily data, which keeps the engineered features defined consistently across tickers regardless of trading calendar variation. With sixty months and twenty tickers, the un-pruned panel contains 1,200 ticker-months; after the rolling-window NA drop described in §4.3, the working panel contains approximately 960 rows.

## 4.2 Macroeconomic series

Four macroeconomic series complement the firm-level data and form the upstream half of the eventual causal graph. Each was chosen for a specific reason: the ECB main refinancing rate captures the policy-rate environment under which euro-area equity valuations are set, the EUR/USD reference rate captures the export-revenue channel that disproportionately affects DAX constituents, the German HICP year-on-year inflation rate captures domestic price-level dynamics, and the CBOE VIX index captures the global risk-aversion regime to which all major equity markets co-move.

The ECB main refinancing rate, the EUR/USD reference rate, and German HICP year-on-year inflation were retrieved from the European Central Bank's statistical data warehouse via its Data API. The VIX series was retrieved through `yfinance` using the `^VIX` ticker. None of the four sources required an API key, which materially simplifies replication: the entire macro panel can be regenerated from a clean Python environment with only the public package dependencies and an internet connection. All four series were resampled, where necessary, to month-end frequency to align with the equity panel.

## 4.3 Feature engineering

Five firm-level features are derived from the monthly OHLCV data and combined with the four macro series to form the nine-feature panel that all subsequent analysis uses [See Table 2]. The five firm features are: rolling realised volatility (twelve-month window of monthly returns), price momentum (six-month price change), average traded volume (six-month rolling mean), one-year total return (twelve-month price change in percent), and maximum drawdown (peak-to-trough decline over a twelve-month rolling window). The choice of windows reflects a balance between responsiveness and noise suppression. A twelve-month window for volatility, return, and drawdown is long enough to estimate second-moment behaviour without being so long that it smooths over regime changes; the six-month window for momentum and volume averages tracks the shorter horizon over which trading-driven signals decay (Jegadeesh and Titman, 1993).

Each rolling computation introduces leading NaNs equal to one less than the window length, which the pipeline then drops on a per-ticker basis. After the drop, the working panel reaches the approximately 960 ticker-months reported above. We retain the four macro series at level for the moment; whether they enter the discovery step at level or in first differences is determined by the stationarity tests of §4.4 rather than assumed in advance.

The nine-feature design is deliberately compact. Earlier iterations of the pipeline experimented with seventeen features, but the additional eight (multiple momentum horizons, RSI, MACD signal, beta-to-market, P/E, US-side macro variables) added redundancy in the correlation matrix without improving holdout accuracy, and they made the discovered DAGs visibly less stable across discovery methods. The final nine-feature specification keeps each feature distinct in economic content (one volatility, one momentum, one liquidity proxy, one realised-return level, one drawdown, plus four macro drivers), which improves the interpretability of the downstream causal-discovery and counterfactual scoring steps without measurable cost in predictive accuracy.

## 4.4 Stationarity diagnostics

Causal-discovery algorithms applied to the level of a non-stationary series tend to find spurious edges driven by trend co-movement rather than by structural dependence. To guard against this we ran the Augmented Dickey-Fuller (ADF) test on each of the nine features over the full panel and recorded the resulting p-value and the implied transformation [See Table 3]. The seven features `volatility`, `momentum`, `volume_avg`, `return_1y`, `max_drawdown`, `vix`, and `eur_usd` rejected the unit-root null at conventional significance levels and were therefore used at level. The two macro series `ecb_rate` and `de_inflation` failed to reject the null (ADF p ≈ 0.92 and p ≈ 0.25 respectively); both were first-differenced before they entered any discovery routine.

The point of this step is narrow but important. The discovered DAG that drives the LEWIS counterfactual scores in Chapter 5 is fitted on the post-transformation panel, so the upstream policy-rate and inflation edges are interpretable as effects of changes in those series rather than artefacts of a shared trend. Reporting the ADF outputs explicitly also makes the diagnostic auditable: a reviewer can rerun the test and verify that the transformation log matches.

## 4.5 Discretisation

For the LEWIS counterfactual evaluation, each continuous feature is binned into ten equal-frequency buckets. Equal-frequency binning is preferred over equal-width binning because it preserves the marginal density of each feature, which makes the back-door adjustment used to compute necessity and sufficiency scores numerically stable even where the underlying distribution is heavy-tailed (Pearl, 2009). Critically, the binning thresholds are fitted on the training partition only and then applied unchanged to the holdout. Fitting bin edges on the full panel before splitting would constitute a subtle form of leakage, because a holdout observation could then influence the bucket edges to which it is later assigned. Restricting the fit to the training set eliminates that channel.

## 4.6 Labelling rule

The supervised target is a binary `investment_decision` flag defined as

investment_decision = 1 if (return_1y / volatility) > 0.5, else 0.

The expression `return_1y / volatility` is a Sharpe-style realised risk-adjusted return; the ratio in the original Sharpe formulation divides excess return by its standard deviation (Sharpe, 1966). Setting the cutoff at 0.5 yields, on this panel, an approximately balanced 50/50 class split, which we chose deliberately so that class imbalance does not confound the comparison between explanation methods. With a heavily skewed target, mean-absolute-SHAP and LEWIS sufficiency scores would each be biased by the prevalence of the majority class in different and not directly comparable ways; an approximately balanced target neutralises that channel.

We emphasise what this label is not. It is a retrospective, realised-Sharpe binary indicator computed at the end of each twelve-month window, not a forward-looking BUY signal in the trading sense. The thesis treats it as a methodological vehicle for studying the causal-explainability properties of a binary classifier whose decision is plausibly investment-relevant; we do not claim that a real portfolio manager would or should commit capital on the basis of this label. Section 4.8 of Chapter 6 returns to this caveat when discussing limitations.

## 4.7 Train/test partition

Two complementary partitions are used. The primary partition is chronological: the last twelve months of the panel (2024) constitute the holdout and the preceding period is the training sample, which gives a roughly 80/20 split by row count (192 rows held out, the remainder used for training). A chronological split is the appropriate stress test in a financial-time-series context because it forecloses the most subtle leakage channel — using the future to predict the past via cross-sectional information shared across tickers within a calendar month.

A secondary, random 80/20 partition is fitted with random seed 42 to bracket the chronological result. Comparing the two diagnoses how much of the holdout performance is attributable to genuine generalisation versus to the absence of regime change between training and test. If the chronological holdout were very much weaker than the random one, that would signal regime sensitivity; if the two are similar, that is reassuring (Lopez de Prado, 2018). Both partitions are evaluated independently in Chapter 5, using the same trained CatBoost model architecture but refit on the relevant training rows.

## 4.8 Expert survey panel

The human-evaluation data come from a structured A/B survey instrument deployed as a Streamlit application and persisted to a MongoDB Atlas collection (with a SQLite local fallback for connectivity outages). At the cut-off used in this thesis the database contained twenty-five completed evaluations [See Table 9]. Every respondent self-identified as a portfolio manager with five years of professional experience; recruitment was through professional networks rather than a probability sample, which we acknowledge as a generalisability constraint in Chapter 6.

Each respondent was randomly assigned a single DAX ticker drawn from the universe of §4.1 and shown two side-by-side explanations of that ticker's predicted investment decision: a LEWIS counterfactual artefact identifying which feature changes would flip the decision, and a SHAP feature-importance plot ranking features by mean absolute attribution (Lundberg and Lee, 2017). Method labels were displayed neutrally as "Method A" and "Method B"; the assignment of A and B to LEWIS or SHAP was randomised across respondents to avoid order-presentation bias. Respondents then answered a fixed set of items: the A/B preference choice, a Likert-scale trust rating on a 1–10 scale, a Likert-scale confidence rating on the same scale, a categorical "mechanics-accuracy" judgement of how well each explanation captured the underlying decision logic, and an open free-text comment field. The descriptive statistics of the responses, together with the demographic breakdown, are reported in Table 9 and analysed in §5.5.

Twenty-five evaluations is a modest panel by survey-research standards and we treat it as such. The OLS regression of trust on explanation predictors reported in §5.6 is therefore best read as a hypothesis-generating exercise rather than a population-level estimate, and we discuss the implications of this sample size for inference in Chapter 6 (Doshi-Velez and Kim, 2017).
